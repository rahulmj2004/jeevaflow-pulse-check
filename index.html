Worked out from heart-rate variability (HRV) in fingertip readings. HRV differs a lot between people, so the page compares each reading with your own usual once you have saved 3 fingertip readings, ideally at the same time of day while resting. Lower HRV than usual suggests more stress or tiredness; it is a wellbeing hint, not a diagnosis.

How to put the cone on the phone

Make the cone

The cone is needed. Without it the chirps spread into the room and the tiny echo from the eardrum is drowned out by the direct sound and by echoes from the face and walls, so the curve comes out flat and would look like fluid.

  1. Download the cone and print it at 100% size. The scale bar should measure 5 cm.
  2. Cut it out and roll it so the narrow end is about 8 mm across. Tape it along the glue tab.
  3. Squeeze the wide end flat over the bottom edge of the phone so it covers both the speaker and the microphone, and tape it to the front and back with no gaps.

Check an ear

  1. Find a quiet room. Set the phone volume to about half and disconnect Bluetooth earphones.
  2. Rest the narrow end gently at the opening of the ear. Never push it into the ear.
  3. Tap Start ear check and keep still for 5 seconds of soft chirps. Stop if the child is upset.

What the curve means

A healthy eardrum vibrates and absorbs part of the sound, which shows as a dip in the curve. Fluid behind the eardrum stiffens it and tends to flatten the dip (University of Washington, Chan et al., Science Translational Medicine, 2019).

The coloured result is a provisional reading of the dip: a clear dip shows green, a flat curve red, and one ear much flatter than the other is pointed out. The cut-offs have not yet been calibrated against ears checked by a doctor, so a green result does not rule out an infection. The curve is most useful for comparing the two ears, or the same ear over days, on the same phone. Curves from different phones cannot be compared. Works best in Chrome on Android; on some iPhones the sound may come from the top earpiece while the microphone is on, and the chirps may not be heard.

How to check

  1. Sit down. Hold the phone loosely in one hand, screen up.
  2. Resting on lap: rest that forearm on your thigh. Arm held out: stretch the arm forward.
  3. Tap Start and stay as still as you can for 20 seconds. Do not grip hard.
  4. Check both hands, in both positions. Parkinson's tremor is usually strongest at rest; essential tremor when the arm is held out.

What the numbers mean

Shake speed is how many times a second the hand shakes. 4–6 is typical of Parkinson's, 4–12 of essential tremor, and 8–12 of the small normal tremor everyone has. Strength is the shaking acceleration, and size the back-and-forth distance it works out to. Steady rhythm shows how much of the shaking is at one speed: a tremor is rhythmic, a fidgeting hand is not.

    About this check

    Face, Arms, Speech, Time (FAST) is the stroke warning sign check used by ambulance services: even one sign that starts suddenly is treated as a possible stroke. Here the phone measures the face (MediaPipe face landmarks), the arms (motion sensor) and speech (Whisper speech recognition and timing), all on this phone. The cut-offs are provisional and have not been checked against patients. Only a hospital scan can confirm a stroke, and a result with no signs does not rule one out. Nothing is uploaded or saved.

    Set up for the night

    1. Plug in the charger. The screen stays on; tap Dim screen to black it out.
    2. Take the phone out of its case. Set the volume to about half and disconnect earphones.
    3. Put the phone on the bed or a bedside table, within about half a metre of your chest, with the bottom edge (the speaker) pointing at you.
    4. Sleep alone in the bed if you can: a partner's breathing mixes in.

    How it works

    The phone plays steady tones at 18–20 kHz and listens to their echo. Each breath moves the chest a few millimetres, which shifts the echo: that wobble is the breathing line above. A flat stretch of 10 seconds or more counts as a pause. Based on ApneaApp (Nandakumar, Gollakota & Watson, MobiSys 2015). Children and pets may hear a faint hiss; stop if it bothers anyone. Sound is processed on this phone and never recorded or uploaded.

    Research preview: not measured here

    Researchers have shown phones can screen for these, but each needs a model calibrated against blood tests on real people. This page does not have that calibration, so it shows no number rather than an invented one.

    Blood oxygen (SpO₂) Not available

    University of Washington researchers estimated oxygen from the fingertip camera and flash in a lab study where volunteers' oxygen was lowered on purpose under medical supervision (Hoffman et al., npj Digital Medicine, 2022).

    Needs: a model trained on paired pulse-oximeter data for each phone type. An uncalibrated "98%" could falsely reassure someone who is unwell. Use a pulse oximeter, and seek care if you are breathless.

    Anaemia (low haemoglobin) Not available

    UW's HemaApp screened haemoglobin from how flash light passes through a fingertip, without a needle (Wang et al., UbiComp 2016).

    Needs: calibration against blood haemoglobin tests, and in the study extra light sources. A blood test at a health centre is the reliable check.

    Newborn jaundice Not available

    UW's BiliCam photographed a baby's skin next to a printed colour card to estimate bilirubin (de Greef et al., UbiComp 2014).

    Needs: the printed card and a model trained on newborns' blood bilirubin results. If a baby's skin or eyes look yellow, especially in the first week, see a doctor or health worker the same day.
