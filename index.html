Measure your pulse with your phone's camera in 30 seconds.

Measure live

  1. Sit down and rest for a minute.
  2. Cover the rear camera and the flash with your index fingertip. Press lightly.
  3. Keep still for 30 seconds.

Or analyse a video

  1. In your camera app, switch the flash to on in video mode.
  2. Cover the lens and flash with your fingertip and record 35 seconds.
  3. Tap Analyse a video and pick that recording.

Measure live

  1. Face a window or a lamp so your face is evenly lit.
  2. Rest the phone on a table or hold it steady at arm's length.
  3. Keep your face in the oval and stay still, without talking, for 30 seconds.

Or analyse a video

  1. Record a 35-second selfie video in good light, keeping still.
  2. Keep your face in the middle of the frame.
  3. Tap Analyse a video and pick that recording.

Face readings give heart rate only. Beat timing from a face is too blurry for HRV, so stress uses fingertip readings.

Stress score

Worked out from heart-rate variability (HRV) in fingertip readings. HRV differs a lot between people, so the page compares each reading with your own usual once you have saved 3 fingertip readings, ideally at the same time of day while resting. Lower HRV than usual suggests more stress or tiredness; it is a wellbeing hint, not a diagnosis.

Check how the eardrum echoes sound, with a paper cone and 5 seconds of chirps.
Download paper cone

Make the cone

  1. Download the cone and print it at 100% size. The scale bar should measure 5 cm.
  2. Cut it out and roll it so the narrow end is about 8 mm across. Tape it along the glue tab.
  3. Tape the wide end over the bottom edge of the phone so it covers both the speaker and the microphone.

Check an ear

  1. Find a quiet room. Set the phone volume to about half and disconnect Bluetooth earphones.
  2. Rest the narrow end gently at the opening of the ear. Never push it into the ear.
  3. Tap Start ear check and keep still for 5 seconds of soft chirps. Stop if the child is upset.

What the curve means

A healthy eardrum vibrates and absorbs part of the sound, which shows as a dip in the curve. Fluid behind the eardrum stiffens it and tends to flatten the dip (University of Washington, Chan et al., Science Translational Medicine, 2019).

This page has not been calibrated against ears checked by a doctor, so it shows the curve but does not say whether there is fluid. It is most useful for comparing the two ears, or the same ear over days, on the same phone. Curves from different phones cannot be compared. Works best in Chrome on Android; on some iPhones the sound may come from the top earpiece while the microphone is on, and the chirps may not be heard.

Research preview: not measured here

Researchers have shown phones can screen for these, but each needs a model calibrated against blood tests on real people. This page does not have that calibration, so it shows no number rather than an invented one.

Blood oxygen (SpO₂) Not available

University of Washington researchers estimated oxygen from the fingertip camera and flash in a lab study where volunteers' oxygen was lowered on purpose under medical supervision (Hoffman et al., npj Digital Medicine, 2022).

Needs: a model trained on paired pulse-oximeter data for each phone type. An uncalibrated "98%" could falsely reassure someone who is unwell. Use a pulse oximeter, and seek care if you are breathless.

Anaemia (low haemoglobin) Not available

UW's HemaApp screened haemoglobin from how flash light passes through a fingertip, without a needle (Wang et al., UbiComp 2016).

Needs: calibration against blood haemoglobin tests, and in the study extra light sources. A blood test at a health centre is the reliable check.

Newborn jaundice Not available

UW's BiliCam photographed a baby's skin next to a printed colour card to estimate bilirubin (de Greef et al., UbiComp 2014).

Needs: the printed card and a model trained on newborns' blood bilirubin results. If a baby's skin or eyes look yellow, especially in the first week, see a doctor or health worker the same day.
